Normal and Lognormal: The Shape of Returns

Prices can't go negative, but returns can. The normal distribution and its exponential cousin, the lognormal, and the moment-generating trick that produces the famous −σ²/2.

November 15, 2026By Diganta Bhattacharya5 min read

The normal distribution

$X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ has density

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).$$

We write $N(x)$ for the standard normal CDF, $N(x)=\mathbb{P}(Z\le x)$ with $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The Black–Scholes formula is literally built out of two evaluations of $N$. Useful facts:

  • Standardising: $X=\mu+\sigma Z$, so $\mathbb{P}(X\le x)=N\!\big(\tfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)$.
  • Symmetry: $N(-x)=1-N(x)$.
  • Sums of independent normals are normal: means add, variances add.

The moment-generating function

The single most useful identity in this series:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\theta X}\big]=\exp\!\Big(\theta\mu+\tfrac12\theta^2\sigma^2\Big)\qquad X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2).$$

Proof sketch. Complete the square in the exponent: $\theta x-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}=-\frac{(x-\mu-\theta\sigma^2)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\theta\mu+\frac{\theta^2\sigma^2}{2}$. The remaining integral is a normal density shifted by $\theta\sigma^2$, which integrates to 1. ∎

Remember the shiftMultiplying a normal density by $e^{\theta x}$ just shifts its mean by $\theta\sigma^2$. That is Girsanov's theorem in miniature (Lecture 9), and it's where $d_1 = d_2+\sigma\sqrt T$ comes from (Lecture 11).

Why prices are lognormal

Suppose the continuously compounded return over $[0,T]$ is normal:

$$\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0}\sim\mathcal N\!\Big(\big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2\big)T,\;\sigma^2T\Big).$$

Then $S_T=S_0e^{X}$ is lognormal. It's always positive, right-skewed, and can't fall below zero, which fits limited liability for a stock. Over many small periods, returns multiply, so log-returns add, and by the CLT a sum of small log-returns is roughly normal.

The −σ²/2 puzzle

Using the MGF with $\theta=1$:

$$\mathbb{E}[S_T]=S_0\,e^{(\mu-\frac12\sigma^2)T+\frac12\sigma^2T}=S_0e^{\mu T}.$$

So the mean price grows at $\mu$, but the median, $S_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}$, grows more slowly. A few huge winners pull the mean up while the typical path lags behind. This is volatility drag: a +50% year followed by a −50% year leaves you at 75%, not 100%.

Raise σ in the figure: the mean stays put while the median slides left and the right tail stretches out. The −σ²/2 term will reappear when we apply Itô's lemma to $\ln S$ in Lecture 8. Then it won't be an assumption but a consequence of the calculus.

Two quantities we'll need later

For $S_T$ lognormal as above, and with $d_2=\frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}$:

$$\mathbb{P}(S_T \gt K)=N(d_2),\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[S_T\mathbf 1_{\{S_T \gt K\}}\big]=S_0e^{\mu T}N(d_2+\sigma\sqrt T).$$

The second follows from the "shifted mean" remark. Replace μ by the risk-free rate r, discount, and these two lines are the Black–Scholes formula. We'll derive that properly in Lecture 11.

Key takeaways

  • N(x), the standard normal CDF, is the building block of the Black–Scholes formula.
  • MGF: E[e^{θX}] = e^{θμ + θ²σ²/2}. Exponential tilting shifts a normal's mean.
  • Lognormal prices: mean grows at μ, median at μ − σ²/2. The gap is volatility drag.
