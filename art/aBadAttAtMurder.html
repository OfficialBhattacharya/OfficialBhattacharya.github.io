Chapter 1: The Calm before the Storm

Part 1

It was a bone-chillingly cold night, which is not a sentence anyone expects to write about Kolkata in July. July in Kolkata is supposed to be a steam bath. That evening it felt more like a refrigerator someone had forgotten to close. Storm clouds had been gathering since the afternoon, and by seven the whole campus had been swallowed by an early, sulking darkness. The wind came howling through the trees without mercy, and the streetlights above the murky pond flickered as if they were having second thoughts about staying on.

On the far side of the pond stood the old graduate hostel, abandoned since the eighties, with its peeling paint and its broken window panes. On nights like this, its reflection in the lamplit water looked less like a building and more like a rumour.

The end-semester exams had finally ended, and most students had fled home for the summer break as fast as trains and buses would carry them. Only a handful remained. Some had stayed back to reappear for compensatory exams. Others, for reasons they couldn't quite explain to their parents, simply preferred the familiar walls of the hostel to anywhere else in the world.

Ashani belonged to the second group. He was a final-year Masters student at our institute, a place built, brick by brick, for people who think in theorems and dream in distributions. That evening he was sitting in the club room, waiting for his friends to come back from the market with supplies, staring out of the window at the gloomy reflection on the water. He was so lost in his thoughts that when a familiar meow came from right behind him, he nearly jumped out of his chair.

It was, of course, Midnight.

Midnight was his pitch-black feline companion, and she had a talent for appearing out of nowhere at exactly the moment you had stopped expecting her. She nudged his legs, meowing tenderly, in the tone of someone who has been kept waiting and would like an explanation. Ashani laughed softly and gave her the pat she was demanding. On days without classes, Midnight usually stayed with him. On all other days she patrolled the entire hostel, watching everyone from a distance, like a sniper surveying enemy territory.

The two of them were still admiring the reflection when Vishal, Akshay and Kriti came back, dripping and triumphant, with bags of supplies for the night.

After a month of exams, tonight was meant to be a night of pure, uncomplicated fun. Fifteen people were staying back who had no exams to write and no classes to miss for a month, and all fifteen intended to sing badly, eat too much and make the kind of hostel memories that get retold, with improvements, for years. The planning was in the hands of Abhay, one of Ashani's batchmates and his childhood friend from school. Abhay's plan was simple and ambitious: everyone meets at eight, plays games, cooks up a storm, drinks as if there is no tomorrow, and carries on until dawn. The last hostel party had been so long ago that nobody could remember it properly, so this one had to be better. To that end, three juniors, Nitin, Soham and Soumya, who had finished their exams a day before everyone else, had been put in charge of turning the club room into something worthy of Diwali.

Part 2

Aabesh and Akash played for the college football team, but their real fame in the hostel came from the kitchen. After every tournament, win or lose, the two of them cooked for the whole team and for anyone who had come out to cheer, which was a very effective way of making sure people came out to cheer. Alok and Shiva, both first-years, were the most enthusiastic of those supporters. They were the only freshers in the hostel that summer, which meant that everyone else was their senior and everyone else reminded them of it. The four of them, together with Urja and Sonam from the girls' hostel, had been given the job of setting up the terrace for a barbecue. Akshay and Abhay were in charge of the games. Kriti and Vishal were in charge of music and drinks, a combination that everyone agreed was either inspired or dangerous. And Ashani and Midnight had been given the most important job of all: buying the charcoal. Without charcoal, there is no barbecue, and without a barbecue, Aabesh had warned us, there would be no Aabesh.

The whole plan had been drawn up a week before exams began, when everyone was still optimistic, and everyone had promised to do their part.

Before I go any deeper into the story, and into everything that happened over the days and weeks that followed, I should introduce myself, and tell you a little about our hostel. If you have been counting the characters so far, and if you are the sort of reader who counts, you will have noticed that one of the fifteen is missing. That is me. My name is Ritwik. I am Ashani's batchmate, his bathroom-mate and Midnight's partner in crime. My job for the night was to help Ashani and Midnight, because I was the only one who knew where the charcoal shops were in the local market, and the only one who knew which of them actually had charcoal.

Apart from the fifteen of us, seven more students had stayed back, all with exams the following week. The plan was to pack their food and deliver it to their rooms, since nobody with an exam in a few days wants to lose an entire night to a party, however good the chicken is. Most of them will drift in and out of this story without doing anything more dramatic than eating their dinner, but one of them you should meet now, because you will be seeing him again.

Tanmoy lived on the second floor. He was a year junior to us, thin and serious, with round glasses that spent most of their life sliding down his nose. In his first year, he had been Rahulda's mentee. Rahulda had taken him under his wing the way good seniors sometimes do, lending him books, checking his proofs and letting him sit in his room until three in the morning arguing about mathematics. Tanmoy had worshipped him. When Rahulda died, Tanmoy stopped speaking to almost everyone for a whole semester. Even now, two years later, he flinched whenever someone said Rahulda's name too loudly.

Of the fifteen of us staying for the party, there are a few you should know a little better too.

Vishal had been the football captain until last semester, when he lost the captaincy over a disciplinary complaint that he still refused to discuss. He was big, loud and generous to a fault, the first to lend you money and the last to leave any party. He also had a temper that could flare up like a matchstick, out of nowhere and all at once. During one match against the PhD team, he had kicked the ball so hard at the referee's back that it took three of us to calm him down afterwards, and the referee is still, I believe, a little afraid of him.

Aabesh, apart from being the best cook in the hostel, owned a canvas roll of six knives that he had inherited from his grandfather, who had once been a chef at a hotel on Park Street. Each knife had a black handle and its own pocket in the canvas. Aabesh carried them to every barbecue and allowed nobody to touch them. Akash was permitted to look at them. Everyone else was permitted to look at Akash looking at them.

Abhay, as I've said, was Ashani's childhood friend, and the kind of person who organises other people's lives because he can't bear to sit still in his own. Akshay was the exact opposite: slow, kind and gentle, the sort of person who called his parents at the same time every single night and had never once, in four years, been heard to say an unkind word about anyone.

And then there was Kriti. But Kriti deserves more than a paragraph, and she will get one soon enough. If I don't give her one, she will write it herself.

Our campus sits near the city limits of Kolkata and is split into three sub-campuses connected by two subways. The hostel campus has several buildings, the staff quarters, a football field and two ponds. Seen from above, the boys' hostel looks like the number 8 on a digital clock, and that is where most of this story takes place. I've added a rough sketch below, along with the best description I can manage.

Both hostels had single rooms, and every two rooms shared a bathroom, which could only be entered from those two rooms. Each room had its own balcony, which people used for drying clothes, and which some had turned into kitchens and a few, more ambitious, into small gardens. Each room had three doors, one to the corridor, one to the balcony and one to the shared bathroom, and a double-panel window right next to the balcony door. The furniture was the same everywhere: a bed, a chair, a desk, a bookshelf and a built-in wardrobe. Both hostels had four floors and beautiful terraces.

Two more things about the boys' hostel, because they will matter later. First, the long corridors were swept about once a month, if we were lucky, so a fine grey layer of dust was always settled along them, recording everyone's comings and goings like a diary nobody read. Second, there was exactly one washing machine in the entire building, in a small laundry room on the ground floor, and when it ran, it rattled loudly enough to wake the whole wing. It was the only appliance in the hostel that everyone could hear and nobody could ignore.

I shared my bathroom with Ashani, and therefore with Midnight, which made the three of us neighbours of a very particular kind.

Part 3

It was around half past seven when I went and knocked on Ashani's door to collect him for the charcoal run, before every shop in the neighbourhood pulled down its shutters. Nobody answered. After a few more knocks, I gave up. On a night like this, there was only one place he could be: the club room, admiring the weather as if it had been arranged for him personally.

I was right. When I walked in, Ashani and Midnight were sitting side by side at the window, both staring at the reflection of the old hostel in the pond, which had once, long ago, been a well. Around them the club room was in organised chaos. Abhay and Akshay were arguing about the order of the games, and Kriti and Vishal were directing Nitin, Soham and Soumya on where every single string of lights should go, mostly by contradicting each other.

I told Ashani that the weather was clearly not going to improve out of respect for us, and that we should get to the market before the charcoal shops closed. We set off, with Midnight trotting along behind. She loved going for walks, especially on windy nights, and especially, I suspected, when the walk might end near food. At the hostel gate, she sat down to wait for us with great dignity while we looked for an auto.

Just as we crossed the road, we ran into Abhik and Saswata, both from our batch, both carrying a thick stack of photocopies of their professor's notes, and both looking like men walking to their own trial. They had a Topology compensatory paper in a few days. To understand why that was so serious, you need to know a little about our college.

Our institute was one of the most respected places in the country for Mathematics and Statistics. It admitted only thirty undergraduates and twenty Masters students a year, paid every one of them a generous monthly scholarship and charged almost nothing for the hostel. Most of its alumni ended up as professors at Ivy League universities or in very comfortable chairs at very large companies. The institute protected this reputation with one simple, merciless rule. You could fail an exam once. If you failed it, you took a compensatory exam. And if you failed that, you were expelled. No appeals, no second chances, no sad emails to the dean. Everyone knew the rule, which is why nobody ever wanted to be standing where Abhik and Saswata were standing now.

Part 4

Abhik's path to that spot was a little unusual. He picked things up so quickly that, early on, he discovered he could pass any exam by studying only on the night before. Having discovered this, he concluded, with flawless logic, that there was no need to study on any of the other nights. This worked beautifully for three years. Then the final semester arrived, with courses that were difficult for everyone, and Abhik's flawless logic finally met a counterexample.

Saswata was a different story. He was talented, but he was also one of the hardest-working people I knew. He came from a difficult household where his uncle had supported the entire family, and when his uncle died while Saswata was still in school, the weight had landed on him. Ever since, he had been looking after his mother and sisters with his scholarship and helping with what was left of the family business. How he managed all of that in his final semester, I still don't know. And yet he had always done better than most of the students in a college where "most of the students" were very good indeed.

Ashani, for his part, was one of the toppers of our batch, and one of those rare toppers who would actually stop and help you. He was talented and efficient, but what impressed me most was his habit of observing and collecting data about everything before coming to any conclusion at all. He didn't guess. He counted. He even carried a cloth measuring tape in his pocket, everywhere, on the grounds that you cannot understand a space until you know its dimensions. I once caught him measuring the width of the mess tables during lunch, and when I asked why, he looked at me as if I had asked why he was breathing. If I had to describe him in one sentence, I would say that Ashani approached life as if it were a statistics problem, and was usually right.

Abhik asked Ashani if he happened to have Rahulda's notes, since Rahulda had been our Topology professor's favourite student, and his notes were said to be almost as good as the professor's lectures. Some said better.

"You only need the textbook, believe me,"

Ashani said.

"But Saswata, didn't you find Rahulda's notes in his room when you moved in last year?"

Saswata nodded.

"Only a few. The Topology ones were missing. His notes were incredible, though. I still don't understand how he did it, you know."
"Maybe we'll never know, ahahaha,"

said Ashani.

I tugged at his sleeve and pointed out that the shops would close in fifteen minutes and that, unless he planned to cook the chicken with Topology notes, we needed to leave.

"You two are coming for a while, right?"

I asked, as Ashani began waving frantically at every auto that passed.

"Of course. I won't miss it. I'll be there after ten,"

said Abhik, waving back.

"I'll drag Saswata along too. Oh look, Ashani's caught one. Go, go, or you won't get your charcoal."

We hurried, sure that we were already too late. In the auto, as it bounced and splashed along the road, Ashani turned to me.

"Have you ever wondered why Rahulda did it? I've never been able to come up with a reasonable explanation. He had multiple PhD offers. He had a PPO from one of the best investment banks in India. There was no girlfriend, as far as anyone knew, no family trouble that we ever heard of. Why would someone like that kill himself, all of a sudden?"
"There's a lot we don't know, Ashani. He had very few friends. His room stayed locked for over a year after he died, and only last year did Saswata move in. Saswata shares a bathroom with Abhik, and Abhik shares everything with everyone, which is the only reason I've heard anything at all. Apparently all Saswata found in there was a few notes and a broken guitar. And nobody's ever heard of a suicide note either."
"Yes, but doesn't that strike you as strange? A student in his final semester, with everything going for him, gets up in the middle of a class, says he needs the restroom, and instead goes up to the tenth-floor terrace of the academic building and jumps. In broad daylight. On a Friday."

He frowned out of the window at the rain.

"And how did he even get onto that terrace? That door at the top of the academic building is always locked. I've tried it."
"Someone must have left it open,"

I said.

"Maybe,"

said Ashani, in the voice he used when he meant no.

"And what's so important about Friday?"

I asked.

"Friday just doesn't seem like a day to kill yourself, ahahaha. Monday, I could understand. Everyone hates Monday. And statistically, among people who are awake, suicide rates are highest at night. Rahulda was a night person. You know that. We all know that. And he chose a Friday afternoon?"
"Fair. But maybe it had something to do with weekends. Being alone in the hostel when everyone else goes home. Or maybe there was something he had to face on weekends, something he couldn't bear. There are a lot of possibilities we can't rule out."

Before we could rule out any of them, the auto stopped outside the shop. Ashani asked the driver to wait so that we could go back in the same auto, and by some miracle, the shop was still open and still had charcoal. We bought enough to cook for an army, loaded it into the auto, and talked about Rahulda the whole way back, going round and round the same questions, without getting a single step closer to an answer.

Chapter 2: The Party Begins

Part 1

By the time the auto dropped us back at the hostel, it was already a quarter past eight. The clouds had grown so dark that they seemed to be sitting on the treetops, and the first fat drops of rain had begun to fall, each one making a small, perfect ring on the surface of the pond. Midnight took one look at the sky, decided that she had not signed up for this, and shot towards the hostel entrance like a black bullet. Ashani and I followed with the charcoal, a little less gracefully. Even from the stairs, we could hear music and laughter spilling out of the club room.

I have to admit, the juniors had outdone themselves.

An hour ago, the club room had been its usual self: dim, damp and faintly smelling of old newspapers. Now it glowed. Strings of warm yellow Diwali lights ran across the ceiling and down the walls, throwing little dancing shadows everywhere, so that the whole place looked like a pandal on the last night of Durga Puja. Someone had dragged cushions and mattresses onto the floor to make a sort of seating area, and to make room for them, the long dark blue runner that usually lay in front of the television had been rolled up and pushed against the wall. It was a synthetic thing, three feet wide and long enough to trip over from any direction, and it had clearly already had an eventful evening. One end of it carried a fresh brown stain the size of a dinner plate, and Kriti was scolding Soham about it as we walked in.

"It's a rug, Kriti-di. It's been through worse,"

Soham protested.

"It has been through you, which is the worst thing that has ever happened to it,"

said Kriti, without looking up from her phone.

Her phone was connected to Vishal's portable speaker, and the room was full of an unpredictable mix of Hindi and English songs, chosen, as far as I could tell, entirely according to Kriti's mood from one minute to the next. Vishal himself was behind the makeshift bar in the corner, lining up bottles and glasses with the seriousness of a surgeon laying out instruments. For a moment, seeing everyone together, laughing and shouting over each other, I almost forgot the conversation we'd had in the auto.

"Finally! We thought the storm had eaten you,"

Abhay called out, spotting us in the doorway. He was wearing a bright red shirt, the kind of shirt only a host would dare to wear, and he looked thoroughly pleased with himself.

"The shop was about to close, but we got enough charcoal for the whole night,"

Ashani said, setting the heavy bag down by the door with a grunt.

"How's it going upstairs?"
"Aabesh and Akash are already up there with the girls, getting the barbecue ready. They've put a tarp over it in case the rain gets serious. Alok and Shiva are helping with the chairs,"

Kriti replied, pausing her playlist just long enough to say it.

Part 2

Since you will be seeing a great deal of Kriti in the pages ahead, I should tell you about her now, before she tells you herself. Which she would. At length.

Kriti Sanyal was our batchmate, doing her Masters in Statistics like Ashani and me, and she was the only person I have ever met who could win an argument with Ashani. Not every time. But often enough to make it interesting. She lived in the girls' hostel, but she spent so many hours in our club room that half the juniors honestly believed she lived here. She was small and quick, with short hair that she cut herself, badly, and refused to let anyone else touch. And she had a way of looking at you while you were talking, as if she were reading a second, smaller line of text printed just underneath your words.

She got that from her father.

Her father was Deputy Commissioner Pradip Sanyal, of the Detective Department of the Kolkata Police at Lalbazar. If you grew up in Kolkata and ever read a newspaper, you have probably heard of him. He was the officer who had cracked the Salt Lake jewellery murders when Kriti was in school, along with half a dozen other cases that had sat on the front pages for weeks. Kriti had grown up with case files on the dining table, photographs she was not supposed to look at, and a father who, at breakfast, would ask her which of last night's two dinner guests had been lying, and how she knew.

She had chosen Statistics, she once told us, precisely so that she would never become a police officer. She hated, more than anything in the world, being introduced as "the DCP's daughter." But she couldn't switch it off. She noticed things about people that the rest of us walked straight past. Ashani noticed patterns. Kriti noticed people. They had been arguing about which was more useful since our very first semester, and neither had ever conceded a single point.

"You're doing it again,"

she said now, watching Ashani's eyes move slowly around the room.

"Doing what?"
"Counting. You've been in this room for thirty seconds and you're already counting something. What is it this time?"

Ashani had the decency to look embarrassed.

"Bulbs. There are two hundred and twelve on those strings. Nine of them don't work."
"See? This is exactly what I mean. Normal people walk into a party and see a party. You walk into a party and see a dataset."
"And what do you see?"

Kriti glanced around the room. It took her about two seconds.

"I see that Vishal is pouring with his left hand, because he's done something to his right one and doesn't want anyone to ask. I see that Soumya has checked her phone eleven times since I sat down, because someone hasn't texted her back. And I see that you two talked about something serious on the way back, because Ritwik has that face he gets when he's going to write about something later."

I hadn't known I had a face. I tried, unsuccessfully, to stop having it.

"We were talking about Rahulda,"

I admitted.

Something flickered across Kriti's face, there for a second and then gone.

"Of course you were. On a night like this."

She turned the music down a little.

"Baba always says that the cases that bother you most aren't the ones you couldn't solve. They're the ones nobody ever opened. Rahulda's was one of those. Suicide in broad daylight, no note, nothing. They closed the file in a week."
"Did your father ever look at it?"

Ashani asked.

"It wasn't his jurisdiction. It went to the local police station here. He asked about it once, I remember, at dinner. He said something about it didn't sit right with him. But it wasn't his case, and he had twenty others that were."

Right on cue, her phone buzzed. She looked at the screen, rolled her eyes all the way up to the Diwali lights, and answered.

"Yes, Baba. Yes, I'm inside. No, I'm not going out in this. Yes, I know about the storm. I can see the storm. I am looking at the storm right now."

She listened for a while, and her face softened.

"Meghna-di? Really? Since when?"

A pause.

"Okay. Okay, I'll tell her you said hello, if I see her. Bye, Baba. Yes. Yes. Bye."

She hung up and sighed the long sigh of every child with a worried parent.

"He calls every single time it rains. As if I'm still nine. But he did have some news. You remember me talking about Meghna-di?"
"The one who beat your father at chess,"

said Ashani.

"The one who beat my father at chess, and in the departmental exams, and at more or less everything else. Meghna Basu. She was his junior in the Detective Department for six years, and he trained her himself. He says she's the best officer he's ever worked with, which, coming from my father, is roughly the same as being given the Bharat Ratna. She's just been made an inspector and posted to the police station that covers our campus. She started this week."
"So if anything happens tonight,"

said Abhay, who had drifted over to listen,

"your father's favourite student will be the one who comes to arrest us."
"So behave yourselves,"

said Kriti.

"She once arrested her own cousin for jumping a red light."

We all laughed. At the time, it was only a joke.

Part 3

Just then, Midnight reappeared at my feet, wearing her most hopeful expression. She had an uncanny ability to sense food being prepared anywhere in the building, and an even more uncanny ability to be standing in exactly the right spot when a piece of it fell. I picked her up, and she began to purr at once, though her eyes never left the direction of the kitchen, where someone was frying pakoras.

I should probably tell you about Midnight, too, since in many ways this is as much her story as anyone's.

Nobody knows exactly where she came from. Two years ago, in the middle of a monsoon very much like this one, a few weeks after Rahulda's death, Ashani found a tiny black kitten sitting on the steps of the old abandoned hostel by the pond. She was soaked through and no bigger than his palm, and yet she wasn't crying or shivering the way kittens are supposed to. She was just sitting there, perfectly calm, looking at the water, as if she were waiting for someone who was running a little late.

Ashani carried her back inside his jacket and named her Midnight, partly for her colour and partly because that was the hour he found her. Within a week, she had appointed herself the owner of the entire boys' hostel. She took breakfast with the mess cooks, dinner with Ashani, and a second dinner with anyone foolish enough to leave their door open. She slept wherever she pleased and came and went through the balconies as though the building had been designed for her convenience, which, as far as she was concerned, it had.

The juniors, of course, had a story about her. Since she had turned up so soon after Rahulda died, and on the steps of the old hostel of all places, some of them liked to whisper that she was Rahulda's cat, come back to keep an eye on things. Ashani found this hilarious. Midnight, as far as I could tell, found it perfectly reasonable.

What I can tell you for certain is that Midnight noticed things. Not the way Ashani did, with numbers, or the way Kriti did, with people. She noticed them the way animals do, with her nose and her ears and her whiskers, long before any of us had the faintest idea that there was anything to notice. Over the next twenty-four hours, I would learn to watch very carefully where Midnight chose to sit, and what she chose to sniff.

And as for me, I'm the one who writes things down.

I have kept a notebook since I was twelve. My mother gave me the first one, a thin blue exercise book, and told me to write down one thing every day that I didn't want to forget. I never stopped. By the time I came to this institute, I had filled more than forty of them, and somewhere along the way I had started taking photographs too, of everything, on my phone. Ashani teased me about it constantly. He said that one day I would photograph something that actually mattered, and on that day I would finally be forgiven for the eleven thousand pictures of Midnight asleep.

I had no idea, that evening, how soon that day was coming.

"Shall we go up, then? I want to see what they've done to the terrace,"

I said, with Midnight still settled comfortably in my arms.

Part 4

On the way to the staircase, I noticed that the hostel, which usually spent its vacations in a sort of dusty coma, had come alive. Doors that normally stayed shut were wide open. People wandered from room to room with plates and glasses in their hands. For one night, it seemed, everyone had agreed to forget about exams, careers and the future in general, and the mood was infectious.

Kriti came up with us, leaving her phone and speaker in Vishal's care, with strict instructions not to touch her playlist under any circumstances. Ashani climbed ahead of us, and as he went, I could see his lips moving silently.

"Sixty-eight,"

he announced when we reached the top.

"Sixty-eight steps from the club room to the terrace. I've counted them every time for four years, and it's never once been sixty-seven or sixty-nine. There's something very reassuring about that."
"There's something very worrying about that,"

said Kriti.

"Stairs don't change, Ashani."
"Exactly. That's what's reassuring. And when something that never changes suddenly does, you know at once that something's wrong."

Kriti shook her head at him, but I saw her smile.

Still, somewhere on those sixty-eight steps, I had picked up a feeling I couldn't quite shake off, the feeling that tonight was different in some way I couldn't name. Maybe it was the strange weather. Maybe it was our conversation about Rahulda, still circling in my head. Whatever it was, Ashani seemed to feel it too. On the way up, he had kept glancing out of each stairwell window towards the academic building, whose tenth-floor terrace stood dark against the clouds. The place where Rahulda had jumped two years ago. Even Kriti, who didn't believe in anything she couldn't see, had followed his gaze once, and then quickly looked away.

Then we stepped out onto the terrace, and for a while I forgot all about it.

Despite the threatening sky, Aabesh and Akash had worked a small miracle. A barbecue pit stood in the centre, with chairs and low tables arranged in a circle around it, and the whole thing was sheltered under a big blue tarp that flapped and grumbled in the wind like an unhappy sail. String lights ran along the railings, swaying gently. Urja and Sonam were setting out plates and cutlery on one of the tables, and Alok and Shiva were wrestling with an extension cord that seemed determined not to reach the lights.

"This looks amazing! You've really outdone yourselves,"

Ashani said, and for the first time that evening, his face lost its faraway look.

Midnight wriggled out of my arms, dropped onto the wet floor, and walked straight across the terrace to the railing on the far side, the one that faced the academic building. There she sat, tail wrapped neatly around her paws, perfectly still, staring out into the dark.

"What's she looking at?"

Kriti asked.

"Nothing,"

said Ashani.

"She does that."

But he went and stood beside her anyway, and after a moment Kriti joined him, and after another moment, so did I. Three of us and a cat, side by side at the railing, looking out at the storm. I remember thinking that it would make a good photograph. I didn't take it. I wish I had.

Then Aabesh shouted that the first skewers were ready, and the spell broke, and the party began properly. Looking around at all those cheerful faces in the glow of the string lights, I wanted very much to believe that the night would pass as peacefully as everyone hoped. But the storm, the darkness and the questions about Rahulda that none of us could answer had made a perfect stage for something unexpected.

Little did we know that by morning, everything would have changed, and this night would become a turning point in all our lives.

Chapter 3: The Uninvited Guest

Part 1

By half past nine, the terrace had become a small island of light in the middle of the storm. The rain had settled into a steady drizzle that drummed on the tarp above the barbecue pit, and the smell of charcoal smoke and roasting chicken had drifted so far down the staircase that, I'm told, the security guard at the gate had started looking up hopefully. Aabesh worked the grill like a man possessed, turning skewers with one hand and shouting instructions at Akash with the other, while Alok and Shiva ran plates between the grill and the tables like waiters at a wedding where the bride's father had not yet paid.

Chapter 4: The Impossible Ghost

He was barely sixteen, as thin as a broomstick, and he knew more about the people in our hostel than any of us knew about ourselves. But Rajat came out, lit his cigarette, and then, for no reason at all, turned round and looked straight into the dark where Saswata was standing.

"I panicked,"

he said, staring at the floor.

"I didn't think. And then he ran. I thought he'd fall, there on the landing. But he ran, and I heard the door slam and the bolt go across, and I just stood there. I don't know how long. Then I rolled up the rug, with my shirt inside it, because that was the plan, and I'd made a plan, and I didn't know what else to do."

He had washed the knife in the laundry sink while the machine ran, slipped it back into Aabesh's roll in the club room while Vishal and Kriti and the juniors slept a few feet away, and hung the rug on the balcony. Then he went back to his room, stood under the shower until the water ran cold, and sat on his bed until it was light.

"When Bapi started shouting this morning,"

he said,

"I thought I'd feel relieved. That Rahulda could rest. But I didn't feel anything at all. I just kept seeing that door close."

He reached into his bag and took out a thick roll of paper tied with a faded rubber band. He held it in both hands for a moment, then held it out to Ashani, and Ashani added the single sheet to it, very carefully.

"Everything's in there. All his drafts. All the dates. Everything he wrote about Rajat. And now that. I want it to go to someone who'll make sure people know it was his. Not mine, not Rajat's. His. Will you do that?"
"I'll do that,"

said Ashani.

"I promise."

Kriti held out her hand.

"Give me a copy of that last page,"

she said.

"Front and back. My father is going to want to read it tonight."

Part 5

We heard the jeep before we saw it. The low growl of an engine, struggling through water, and then a long blast of a horn from the direction of the main gate.

Through the club room window, I watched it come. A white police jeep with a blue light on top, pushing slowly through the floodwater on the main road, its wheels throwing up brown waves on either side. It stopped at the hostel gates. Two officers climbed out. One was a constable, who stood looking at the flooded path to the hostel as if it had personally insulted him. The other was a woman in khaki, tall, with her hair tied back tightly and a notebook already in her hand, who did not stop to look at the water at all. She simply rolled up her trousers and started wading toward us. Behind them came Mr. Ghosh, hurrying out of his office, still in the pyjamas he had been wearing since morning, with his ring of keys jangling.

"Meghna-di,"

Kriti said, under her breath.

Saswata watched them for a moment. Then he gently lifted Midnight from his lap and set her down on the cushion beside him. She looked up at him, and for once in her life, she did not complain about being moved.

"Look after her,"

he said to Ashani.

"She likes the fish from the Thursday mess. Don't let Abhik feed her chicken bones."

He stood up and straightened his blue shirt. Abhik stood up with him.

"I'm coming with you."
"Abhik..."
"I'm coming with you to the gate. You can't stop me."

Saswata looked at him for a long moment, and then he nodded. He had reached the door when Ashani spoke again.

"Saswata. One more thing."

Saswata stopped, his hand on the door frame, and turned around.

"I have lived in this hostel for four years,"

said Ashani.

"So has Ritwik. We were here when Rahulda died. We were here for the whole year his room was locked. Neither of us had ever heard of the Blade Ghost until last night. This morning I asked Soham where he first heard it. From Haru-da, the night guard, last monsoon. And Haru-da heard it from a senior on the second floor, who kept telling him about dragging sounds in the corridor at night. Last monsoon. Just weeks after you found those papers."

The room had gone completely silent. I don't think anyone was breathing.

"The Blade Ghost,"

said Ashani quietly.

"Who told it first?"

Saswata looked at him for a long time. Then he looked at Tanmoy, who had heard the dragging sound and kept his door shut, exactly as the story said he should. And then he looked back at Ashani.

"I did,"

he said.

Nobody moved.

"I told Haru-da I kept hearing something being dragged along the corridor at night, near the stairs. I told him twice. Then I told one of the juniors, and I let him tell the others. By the end of the monsoon, they were telling it better than I ever could. They added the shadow, and the blade. I only gave them the sound."
"So that one night,"

said Ashani,

"if anyone on that floor heard something being dragged down the corridor, they'd stay behind their doors."
"A plan you make so that you never have to use it,"

said Saswata.

"I told you. I just made it very carefully."

He turned, and walked out of the club room, and Abhik went with him.

The rest of us followed them as far as the club room door, but no further. We stood in the doorway, Ashani and Kriti and Vishal and Tanmoy and me, and watched them walk out of the hostel and down the flooded path together, side by side, the water up to their ankles, then up to their shins, the morning sun shining on the surface around them. Halfway to the gate, Abhik put his arm around Saswata's shoulders, the way he had in the corridor that morning, and Saswata let him.

They met the woman in khaki halfway, on the flooded path. Saswata said something to her. I couldn't hear what. I saw her look at him for a long moment, and then past him, toward the club room door where we were standing. Then she took Saswata's arm, not roughly, and walked him back to the gate.

Beside me, Ashani let out a slow breath.

"Two hundred and twelve bulbs,"

he said quietly, to nobody in particular.

"Sixty-eight steps. Forty-three cigarettes. I can count anything, Ritwik. I couldn't count my way to stopping this."

At the gate, Mr. Ghosh put both his hands on top of his head and stared.

Saswata didn't look back. He climbed into the back of the jeep by himself.

Abhik stood at the gate until the jeep had turned around and disappeared slowly down the waterlogged road, and for a long time after that.

Midnight had come to sit at Ashani's feet. Ashani bent down and picked her up, still holding Rahulda's notes in his other hand, and for a long time, all of us just stood there in the doorway of the club room, in the warm sunlight, saying nothing at all.

Epilogue

It has been almost two months since that night, and I am writing this at Ashani's desk, because mine is covered in Kriti's files.

I should explain that.

A lot happened in those two months, and I'll tell it quickly, because most of it you can probably guess. Ashani kept his promise. He took Rahulda's notes to the head of the department himself, and when the head of the department hesitated, he sent copies to the journal, to Rahulda's family, and to the two universities that had offered Rahulda a PhD place. The journal has opened an investigation into Rajat's paper. So has the institute, into both of his papers, and into the juniors whose names had quietly disappeared from them. Three of them have already come forward. Professor Sen has been asked to step down as Topology coordinator. Tanmoy's name has been restored to the second paper. Nobody in the department says Rahulda's name in a whisper anymore.

The woman in khaki turned out to be Inspector Meghna Basu. Kriti's Meghna-di, her father's old junior, who had been posted to the police station covering our campus exactly five days before.

It was only later that we found out what Kriti had done that morning. While the rest of us were standing in the corridor panicking about ghosts, she had slipped away to the window and called her father. And her father, who had once said at dinner that Rahulda's case didn't sit right with him, had called Meghna Basu himself. That was why, out of all the officers in north Kolkata, the one who waded through the flood to our gate was her.

She spent most of that afternoon in the warden's office taking our statements, one by one. Mine took twenty minutes. Ashani's took nearly three hours. When he came out, he looked more exhausted than I had ever seen him, even after exams.

"She asked me everything twice,"

he said.

"The width of the strip. The number of cigarette butts. The time the battery died. She wrote it all down, and then she asked me again, to see if the numbers changed."
"Did they?"
"No."

He almost smiled.

"I think she was checking my work."

Kriti's father read the last page that night, front and back, exactly as she had said he would. Within a week, Inspector Basu had pulled the old file on Rahulda's death from the records room of her own police station. Most of it was what we expected: a few statements, a hurried report, a verdict of suicide reached in seven days. But there was also a set of photographs of the tenth-floor terrace, taken the afternoon he died. In one of them, near the parapet, half hidden under a ventilation pipe, there was a single cigarette butt. Nobody had ever asked whose it was. Inspector Basu sent Ashani a photograph of it, enlarged, with no message at all. She didn't need to write one. It was Rajat's brand.

The weather-station key register, when they found it in a drawer of the department office, showed that Rajat had signed out the terrace key at 11:10 that Friday morning, and signed it back in at 12:05. The case has been reopened. It can't end in a trial, because the only person who could stand in the dock is dead. But it will no longer end in the word suicide, either, and Rahulda's family has asked for that more than anything.

Before she left that day, Inspector Basu had stood in the second-floor corridor for a long time, looking down at the clean strip in the dust, which nobody had been allowed to sweep. Then she shook Ashani's hand, said nothing at all, and drove away. We assumed that was the last we would ever see of her.

A week after the arrest, Kriti's father invited Ashani and me to dinner at their flat in Ballygunge. I had expected someone large and loud, the kind of police officer you see in films. Deputy Commissioner Pradip Sanyal turned out to be a thin, quiet man with grey hair and reading glasses, who spoke so softly that you had to lean forward to hear him, and who did not miss a single thing that happened at that table. He let Ashani explain the whole case from beginning to end without interrupting once. Then he asked exactly three questions, and every one of them was about something Ashani had not thought to check.

At the door, as we were leaving, he shook Ashani's hand and held on to it for a moment.

"Numbers are a good servant, young man,"

he said.

"But people are the ones who lie to them. Kriti will teach you that, if you let her. And when the two of you get stuck, which you will, my door is open."

Kriti rolled her eyes all the way down the stairs. But I noticed she didn't argue.

Abhik passed his compensatory, with the second-highest mark in the batch. A different examiner graded it. He visits Saswata every Sunday and brings him books. Saswata's mother has started calling Abhik too, every Wednesday evening, to ask whether her son is eating properly. Abhik always tells her that he is.

Saswata is waiting for his trial. I won't pretend to know how I feel about that. I have tried to write about it several times, and I have deleted it every time. What he did was wrong. What was done to Rahulda was wrong too, and for two years, nobody did anything about it except a quiet scholarship student with a broken guitar, who planned for months, and who told a ghost story so carefully that a whole hostel believed it. I still don't know whether I am more frightened of what he did, or of how patiently he prepared for it. I think about that a lot. I suspect I always will.

The juniors have stopped telling the story of the Blade Ghost. It is strange how quickly a ghost dies once everyone knows who invented it. Tanmoy opens his door now when he hears a noise in the corridor at night. Vishal has his captaincy back, and he has stopped hiding his hands. Kamala-mashi finally swept the second floor, and as far as I know, she still thinks Ashani is mad.

And then there is Kriti.

About three weeks after the jeep came, she walked into the club room one evening while Ashani and I were playing chess and Midnight was asleep on the board, and she sat down opposite us without being asked.

"The battery,"

she said.

"I'm sorry?"

said Ashani.

"Quarter to two. The battery died at quarter to two. You told the whole hostel that's how you timed it. That was my clue. I gave it to you, and I didn't even know I was giving it to you, and you didn't even say thank you."
"Thank you,"

said Ashani.

"Too late,"

said Kriti.

"I'm not interested in a thank you. I'm interested in the next one."
"The next what?"
"The next case. Don't look at me like that, Ashani, there's always a next one. This campus is full of strange things nobody ever explains. And you two clearly need someone who notices things that aren't numbers. You're a statistician, he takes photographs, and your cat does half the actual work. I'm joining."

Ashani looked at me. I looked at Ashani. Then we both looked at Midnight, who had woken up, and who was sitting on the chessboard with a black bishop under her paw, looking at Kriti with an expression of deep and serious consideration.

After a long moment, Midnight got up, stepped off the chessboard, walked across the table, and sat down next to Kriti.

"Well,"

said Ashani,

"that settles it, I suppose."

So now there are four of us. Ashani, who sees the world in distributions and outliers. Kriti, who argues with every one of his conclusions until he proves it twice. Me, with my phone and my notebook, writing it all down. And Midnight, who goes wherever she likes, and who is, if I'm being honest, the best detective of the four of us.

Kriti wanted to call us something serious. Ashani wanted to call us nothing at all. In the end, Abhik painted a small wooden sign for the club room door as a joke, and it stuck.

MIDNIGHT & CO.

Last night, I sat on the club room balcony with Midnight in my lap, the way Ashani had at the beginning of all this. The rug is back on the floor in front of the television now. Someone has already spilled tea on it. The sky was clear, for once, and the moon was full, and the old hostel building lay perfectly still in the dark water of the pond, with nothing in its reflection but the moon.

I was still sitting there when Ashani's phone began to ring inside the club room. He was at the table with Kriti, arguing about something, and he let it ring twice before he looked at the screen. It was a number none of us knew.

"Hello?"

I couldn't hear the other side of the conversation, not at first. I only saw Ashani's face change. He sat up straighter. Then he put the phone down on the table and pressed the speaker button, and waved Kriti and me over.

"Say that again, please. My colleagues are here."

There was a short pause on the line, and then a woman's voice, calm and clipped, the kind of voice that is used to being listened to.

"This is Inspector Meghna Basu. We met two months ago, at your hostel. I read your statement again last week, Mr. Ashani. All eleven pages of it. Twice."
"Pradip-da sends his regards, Kriti. He's the one who told me to call."

Kriti groaned and put her head in her hands.

"I hope the numbers haven't changed,"

said Ashani.

"They haven't. That's why I'm calling."

Another pause. In the background, I could hear traffic, and a radio crackling.

"Three days ago, a man was found dead in a flat in north Kolkata. Every door bolted from inside. Windows barred. Nothing taken, nothing out of place, and no way in or out that my team can find. They have been staring at that room for three days, and the only thing they agree on is that it's impossible."

Kriti and I looked at each other.

"I don't believe in impossible rooms,"

said Inspector Basu.

"And from what I read, neither do you. I'd like to pick your brain. Unofficially, for now. Tomorrow morning, if you're free. I'll send a jeep."

Ashani looked at Kriti. Kriti was already nodding. He looked at me, and I held up my notebook. Then he looked down at the table, where Midnight had jumped up beside the phone and was staring at it, her ears pointed straight forward, her tail moving slowly from side to side.

"Inspector,"

said Ashani,

"would there be room in that jeep for four?"

There was a silence on the line. Then, for the first time, I heard Inspector Basu laugh. Just once, very briefly.

"Bring whoever you like. Eight o'clock."

The line went dead.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Kriti reached across and turned the wooden sign on the door around, so that it faced the corridor.

MIDNIGHT & CO.

I sat down at the table, picked up my notebook, and turned to a fresh page.
